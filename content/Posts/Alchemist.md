Today does not merely mark the presentation of a bachelor thesis; it marks the strange and solemn kind of day in which a person stands before himself. For though universities may call it an academic milestone, I cannot help but see it as something far deeper: the moment when the alchemist, the curer of loneliness, reaches the end of an era of his becoming.

And though a part of me resists this ending—because endings always reveal how much we have taken for granted—I must acknowledge that this moment stands exactly where it must. In the hidden architecture of existence, everything is indeed in its right place, even when we only understand its necessity in retrospect.

Parham, my brother, was the remedy to my loneliness, not in the superficial sense of simply “being there,” but in the Kierkegaardian sense: he became a presence that revealed my own inwardness to me. Yet in a divine irony, he himself had no shadow walking beside him on the long road of time. He carried the scorching light of responsibility, doubt, and becoming upon himself—alone—and yet he extended healing to others.

I watched him, not as one observes a spectacle, but as one witnesses the hidden work of a soul. I saw him shatter into ashes under pressures that no one else fully understood. And then, in the quiet way that only the single individual can rise, he returned—renewed, reorganized, regrown. His becoming was not loud, not celebrated by the crowd, but inward and earnest, and therefore infinitely more beautiful.

To see him arrive at this day—standing ready to defend the labor of his mind and the truth he has wrestled with—is like seeing the first cherry blossom bloom after a relentless winter. Fragile, yes, but also undeniably strong. Tender, yet victorious. A symbol not of the end of struggle, but of the possibility that one can wrest meaning out of existence and place it, gently but firmly, into the world.

Thus, today is not simply his thesis day. It is the quiet culmination of an era of suffering, endurance, and self-transformation. And it is the beginning of another era—one that no teacher, no institution, no spectator can define for him. Only he can step into it, as the single individual who has struggled, chosen, and become.

To my brother Parham: today you are not just presenting a thesis. You are presenting your becoming. And that is a far greater achievement than any title a university could bestow.

Sincerely yours, 
I.M. Aski
