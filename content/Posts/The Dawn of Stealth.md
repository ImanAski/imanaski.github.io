## The Problem: What Happens When Light Hits a Wall?

1. **Geometrical Optics (GO)**

To understand the Ufimtsev's breakthrough we need to understand
the problem he was trying to solve. For centeries scientists have
used **Geometrical Optics (GO)** to model how light and other waves
travel. Think of GO as a "light ray" model. A ray comes in, hits
a mirror and bounces back, Simple right? Well it's good for large 
and smooth surfaces.

But GO has a major flaw; it can't explain shadows. According to 
GO, the region behind an object should be completely dark, with
zero wave energy. But according to experiments (and your life) we
know that this can not be true. Sound waves bend around corners, 
and you can still see a little light in the shadow of a building.
This concept is called **Diffraction**, in which **GO** completely
fails to accound for it.


2. **Physical Optics (PO)**

PO is more physically realistic. It recognizes that an incident 
electromagnetic wave induces a surface current on an object. 
This induced current then re-radiates, creating the scattered 
field. PO approximates this surface current ($J_{PO}$) as being 
equal to the current that would exist on an infinite, flat 
tangent plane at that point.

Mathematically, the PO current on the illuminated surface 
of a perfect conductor is:

$$
J_{PO} = 2 * \hat{A} \times H_{\text{incident}}
$$

where $\hat{A}$ is the unit normal vector to the surface and 
$H_{\text{incident}}$ is the incident magnetic field

Well The key flaw is **PO** is that it **it assumes the surface current 
abruptly drops to zero at the edge** between the illuminated and 
shadowed regions. In reality, this transition is not discontinuous. 
The physics at the edge is far more complex, and this oversimplification
leads to significant errors, especially in calculating the backscatter 
(the signal that returns to a radar source).

And as usual a workaholic communist russian guy could see something
else in this. Ufimtsev knew (he's still alive) that something was
special about edges, and they were not simple passive boundries.

## Ufimtsev's Breakthrough: Isolating the Edges

Ufimtsev's Physical Theory of Diffraction (PTD) was a masterstroke 
of decomposition. He recognized that the total scattered field 
could be expressed as the sum of two distinct contributions:

$$
E_{\text{scattered}} = E_{PO} + E_{\text{edge}}
$$

The first term, $E_{PO}$, is the field calcutated using the standard Physical 
Optics approximation. It accounts for the scattering from the large, flat surfaces.

The second term, $E_{\text{edge}}$, is Ufimtsev's crucial innovation. 
This term represents the field generated by a specific type of surface 
current that exists only at the edges: **the fringe current**. 

Ufimtsev posited that the true surface current ($J_s$) on an object 
could be decomposed as:

$$
J_{s} = J_{\text{PO}} + J_{\text{fringe}}
$$

Here, $J_PO$ is the uniform current on the illuminated surface, and 
$J_{\text{fringe}}$ is the non-uniform, localized current that flows 
along the sharp edge to satisfy the electromagnetic boundary conditions. 
It is this $J_{\text{fringe}}$ that is the source of the edge wave. 

The physics is this: an incident wave forces the tangential component 
of the electric field to be zero on the surface of a perfect conductor. 
At a sharp edge, this condition creates a singularity in the field. 
The material responds by setting up a strong, oscillating current along 
the edge to "cancel out" this singularity. This current, in turn, radiates 
a cylindrical wave that propagates away from the edge, illuminating the 
shadow region and sending energy back toward the radar source.

## The Mathematics of the Edge Wave 

Ufimtsev's genius was not just in identifying the fringe current, but in deriving a practical, analytical way to calculate its contribution. He did this by solving a canonical problem: the diffraction of a plane wave by an infinite, perfectly conducting wedge. 

The solution for this idealized shape could then be applied locally to any edge on a complex body, as long as the radius of curvature of the edge was large compared to the wavelength. 

The scattered field from the edge wave can be expressed in a simplified form as: 

$$
E_{edge(θ_s)} ≈ D(θ_i, θ_s, n) * (e^{(-jkr)} / √r) 
$$

Let's break down this powerful equation: 

- $E_{edge(θ_s)}$: The amplitude of the edge wave field at a scattering angle θ_s.

- $D(θ_i, θ_s, n)$: This is the diffraction coefficient. It is the heart of PTD. This complex-valued function tells you the amplitude and phase of the diffracted wave. It depends on:

- $θ_i$: The angle of the incident wave relative to the edge.

- $θ_s$: The angle of observation (scattering).

- $n$: The wedge angle (e.g., n=2 for a half-plane, n=1.5 for a 90-degree interior corner).
Ufimtsev derived explicit, finite formulas for $D$, successfully avoiding the singularities of GTD.
    
- $(e^{(-jkr)} / √r)$: This term describes the fundamental nature of the radiated wave. 
It is a cylindrical wave emanating from a line source (the edge). 
Notice the $1/√r$ amplitude decay, which is characteristic of a line source, 
as opposed to the $1/r$ decay from a point source (like a reflection from a 
flat plate facet). This was a key physical insight.

By calculating $E_{\text{PO}}$ for the large surfaces and adding $E_{\text{edge}}$
for every edge, engineers could for the first time create a highly accurate, computationally feasible model of an object's Radar Cross-Section (RCS). 
From Equations to the F-117 Nighthawk 

When Lockheed engineers began designing a stealth aircraft in the 1970s, they used a computer program called "ECHO 1" which was based on Ufimtsev's PTD. The theory gave them a quantitative tool to solve the stealth problem: 

1. Faceting: The aircraft was composed of large, flat panels. This allowed them to use 
Geometrical Optics to ensure that the main reflections ($E_PO$) were 
directed away from the threat radar. 

2. Edge Alignment: PTD told them precisely how dangerous edges were. 
Using the diffraction coefficient $D$, they could calculate 
the direction and strength of the edge wave returns. Their solution 
was to align all the leading and trailing edges to be parallel to one of 
just a few directions. This concentrated all the edge wave energy ($E_{edge}$)
into a few narrow "spikes" in the aircraft's radar signature. From almost 
any other angle, the combined RCS was vanishingly small. 

The iconic sawtooth pattern on the engine exhaust and landing gear doors is 
a direct, physical application of PTD. Each tooth is a small wedge, and 
its angles are carefully chosen to direct its edge wave away from a 
monostatic radar. 

## Conclusion: A Legacy in Physics
Pyotr Ufimtsev's Physical Theory of Diffraction stands as a landmark achievement in 
classical electrodynamics. By isolating the physics of the edge wave and 
providing a robust mathematical framework through the diffraction coefficient, 
he solved a long-standing problem. His work demonstrated that the most 
disruptive technologies don't always come from applied engineering, 
but from a deep, fundamental inquiry into the nature of the physical world. 
He wasn't just building equations; he was listening to the way waves "ring" 
at the edge of reality, and in doing so, he changed the shape of modern warfare.
